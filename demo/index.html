How it works

The AI reads. The rules decide.

Five steps from a pile of documents to a verdict you can audit. The only place a language model touches the answer is the reading step, and everything it reads is shown back to you.

You bring

1 · Documents

Paste text, upload a PDF, or photograph a deed. Stamps, handwriting and blur are fine.

AI reads

2 · Reader

Copies facts into records: parties, dates, liens, authority. Never judges. Flags blurry text instead of guessing.

Relate graph

3 · Graph

Assets, parties, transfers, claims, authorities and passages, linked. Every fact carries two dates.

Rules decide

4 · Rule engine

Eighteen named Cypher checks reach one of three verdicts: clean, defective, or inconclusive.

You audit

5 · Proof

The broken link, the rule id, the smallest set of records it rests on, and what would fix it.

One rule keeps this honest.The reader only copies what a record says. The rule engine is deterministic: the same graph always gives the same verdict. A citation the agent invents is rejected, because it must point at stored text.

Two dates

What was true. What was known.

Every fact has two dates: when it took effect and when it was recorded. Slide back in time and the verdict changes with what had been filed. Clean until a late-recorded deed arrives, then defective.

Does the graph help?

We measured it. Including the losses.

Four arms, same model, same evidence, 40 held-out synthetic cases, three repeats each. A graph on its own did not help. A graph plus deterministic checks did.

HARD TIER40 harder cases, written after a rule freeze

Two defects in one chain, sound chains that look suspicious, missing records under a "title is clear" summary, powers that do not cover a sale, contradictory filings. Documents only: 72.5%. Graph with its own Cypher: 50%. Graph with the rule engine: 100%, in all three repeats.

THE SHARPEST RESULT"The title is clear," said the agent

Given a chain with a missing record and a summary saying the title is clear, the documents-only agent answered clean 18 times out of 18. The agent with the rule engine abstained every time and named the missing record.

On real records

30,803 real deeds. Every flag explained.

The same engine, unchanged, on public property records from New York (36 Brooklyn lots) and Philadelphia (about 10,000 properties), each deed with its real signing date, recording date, sellers and buyers.

0real deeds checked, from 10,035 properties in two cities
0of 20,768 sales: the seller is the previous buyer, as the rule expects
0sales with no record anywhere in the public register of how the seller got the property
0deeds recorded over three months after signing. That gap is why every fact has two dates.

MOST FLAGSSpelling, families, foreclosures

Of 2,202 Philadelphia findings: 617 names spelled differently in the register, 244 foreclosures, 231 estates or agents, 210 relatives or sister companies, 262 deeds outside our extract. Each is labelled, and a person confirms; code never guesses a near-miss name.

WORTH CHECKINGGaps the register cannot explain

445 sellers with no recorded acquisition, 119 deeds signed before the previous one, and in New York a titleholder whose name changes year between purchase and sale. These are what a title examiner would chase.

FOUND BY REAL DATABugs in our own code

Single-letter names, apostrophes and the register's 40-character name limit stopped names matching themselves, and one deed is dated the year 206. Fixed and tested; the name fix also cleared New York's one false alarm.

LIMITSWhat it does not show

Deeds only, linked in recorded order. Liens are not checked. A flag means "worth a look", not a proven defect: we did not search beyond the public register. Names and addresses stay out of the repository.

Built on Relate

Ownership is a graph.

“Was the grantor the previous grantee?” and “was this lien released before the sale?” are patterns over relationships, so each rule is a short Cypher query on Relate by Neo4j.

  • Single-file store. vestigium.rl is persistent, one file anyone can open, no server.
  • Isolated graphs. Every benchmark case is its own in-memory graph.
  • Throwaway copies. “What would fix it” rehearses a record on a copy and leaves the real graph untouched.
PROP-AUTH · authority not valid on the signing date
MATCH (t:Transfer)-[:MOVES]->(:Asset {id:$asset}),
      (t)-[:ACTED_UNDER]->(au:Authority)
WHERE /* effective by as_of, recorded by known */
  AND NOT EXISTS { (t)-[:ACTED_UNDER]->(ok:Authority)
        WHERE ok.valid_from <= t.effective
          AND (ok.valid_to IS NULL OR ok.valid_to >= t.effective) }
RETURN t.id AS transfer,
       au.agent + ' signed on ' + toString(t.effective) + ' but authority ran ' + …
       // plus the record ids that prove it AS evidence
What this is, and is not

Read this before you trust a number.

A good benchmark reports what could make it look better than it is.

MOSTLY SYNTHETICSynthetic benchmark, fictional rules

The benchmark uses fictional parties, a fictional jurisdiction and generic rules; only the 30,803 deeds in the real-records section are real. This is a prototype of the idea, not legal advice and not a title product.

CEILING100% is the ceiling, not a claim

The rules and the case generator were written together. That is why the rule engine alone is an arm in the benchmark, and why we test it with invariance checks and fresh seeds too.

SMALLOne small model, 40 cases

Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite on the free tier. A stronger model might narrow the gap for the first two arms. The graph-with-own-Cypher arm swung from 47.5% to 62.5% between repeats.

MEASUREDBad photos can still fool the reader

The model flagged none of its own misreads, so every scan is now read twice and disagreements are flagged: 3 of 5 poor-photo misreads caught, about 1 in 40 false alarms. A mistake both reads share still slips through. Real scans and true handwriting are untested.
